# Shared amenities: what the research says, and an amenity layer for the many-agent world (2026-11-13)

Wording rule: agents feel nothing. "Pleasure" and "suffering" are glosses for a modeled hedonic state (ADR 0010).

Status: a research note, not a result. It is the research step for H43 (experiments/hypotheses.jsonl), logged 2026-11-13 from the author's question "did we run housing stuff and collective pleasure amenities or no". H43 claims that shared amenities raise pleasure with everyone counted and lower domination: they supply pleasures that are cheaper, or only possible, together; they raise contact and making; they lower the cost of being single or leaving, so fewer households are held; and whether they last depends on norms that maintain them and prevent overuse. Nothing here changes code. **Every number in the proposal is ASSUMED unless a source is named next to it**, and named sources set directions or rough sizes, not inputs.

Sources were checked by web search on 2026-11-13. Each one below was confirmed to exist by its DOI, a catalog record or the publisher's page. Most papers were read at abstract level, marked "(abstract)". A few were read in part, marked "(read in part)". Books were not read; what they argue comes from publisher pages, catalog records and reviews, marked "(citation level)". Cost figures that come from news coverage of a report, not the report, are marked "(secondary)".

## 0. Why amenities matter for this repo

**Our own results already point here.** Friend contact is the biggest pleasure lever in the household world (docs/notes/housing-research.md, section 0). Contested exits were conceded by the partner who ran short of money (docs/results/exit-fallbacks.md). The friend-network design makes isolation measurable as exit support (docs/notes/friend-network-design.md, section 7). Each of those can be moved by a place outside the home that is cheap to use and full of people.

**The housing note found one amenity already.** Its regime D, cohousing, is private units plus a common house. The common house is a shared amenity with closed membership. This note widens that to amenities anyone in the world can use.

**The question has three halves.** Do amenities add pleasure and contact? Do they make leaving cheaper? And do they survive their own users? The research says most about the first, a little about the third, and almost nothing direct about the second.

## 1. What the research says, by strand

### Third places

**Oldenburg named the idea and argued for it.** *The Great Good Place* calls cafés, bars, shops and similar spots "third places": informal public gathering places beyond home and work. He describes them as neutral ground that levels status, kept alive by regulars, with conversation as the main activity (Oldenburg 1989, citation level). It is an argument from observation, not a study.

**One national survey found that access goes with perceived quality of life.** In a US survey, people who believed third places were accessible in their community rated community quality of life higher, regardless of where they actually met people (Jeffres et al. 2009, abstract). Cross-sectional and self-reported: perceived access, not measured use.

**In poor neighbourhoods, shops do much of the work.** Interviews with 180 residents of six deprived British neighbourhoods found local third places important for social interaction, shops especially, with importance varying by group and with barriers to entry (Hickman 2013, abstract). Qualitative.

**Solid or weak.** The idea is widely accepted. The evidence that third places cause wellbeing is weak: one survey, one qualitative study, no designs that separate place from the people who choose it.

### Social infrastructure, heat and disaster

**Klinenberg's claim.** Social infrastructure is "the physical places and organizations that shape the way people interact" (Klinenberg 2018, citation level, as quoted in reviews). Libraries are his favourite case. Reviewers praise the why and say the book is weaker on how to build and fund it.

**The Chicago heat wave, case-control evidence.** In the July 1995 heat wave, a case-control study found the strongest risk factors for death were being confined to bed (odds ratio 8.2) and living alone (2.3). Working air conditioning and access to transport were protective (0.3 each), and so were social contacts such as group activities or friends nearby (Semenza et al. 1996, abstract). This is the solid core: isolation killed. It is about people's contacts, not about places.

**The neighbourhood comparison is weaker.** Klinenberg's *Heat Wave* compares two adjacent, similarly poor West Side areas: reviews report 19 deaths in North Lawndale (40 per 100,000) against 3 in Little Village (4 per 100,000), and attribute the gap to empty streets and lost shops in one and busy commercial life in the other (Klinenberg 2002, citation level). A neighbourhood-level analysis found commercial decline went with higher heat-wave mortality, consistent with that story (Browning et al. 2006, abstract). Duneier re-examined the North Lawndale cases and argued the theory did not hold at the individual level: few victims there were elderly people living alone (Duneier 2006, abstract). So the place story has support at area level and a live challenge at person level.

**Disasters: social capital predicts survival and recovery, observationally.** Across 133 municipalities hit by the 2011 Tohoku tsunami, wave height, social capital and political support for the ruling party all predicted mortality (Aldrich & Sawada 2015, abstract). Aldrich's case book (Tokyo 1923, Kobe 1995, Tamil Nadu 2004, New Orleans 2005) argues that well-networked communities recovered faster, and reviewers note that strong networks also excluded outsiders from aid (Aldrich 2012, citation level). A review piece separates bonding ties, which help households cope, from bridging ties, which bring information and resources (Aldrich & Meyer 2015, abstract).

**Solid or weak.** Contact protects in crises: solid, observational. Places produce that contact: plausible, area-level, contested.

### Parks and green space

**The best causal evidence on amenities is here, and it is about vacant lots.** In Philadelphia, 541 vacant lots were randomly assigned to greening, trash cleanup, or nothing. Over 38 months, residents near treated lots reported less perceived crime, fewer safety worries going outside, and more use of outside spaces for relaxing and socializing. Crime fell in neighbourhoods below the poverty line: overall −13.3%, gun violence −29.1% (Branas et al. 2018, abstract). A later citywide analysis found shootings fell 6.8% with greening and 9.2% with cleanup alone (Moyer et al. 2019, read in part).

**The same trial measured mental health.** Residents near greened lots reported feeling depressed 41.5% less than controls (95% CI −63.6% to −5.9%), with a larger drop below the poverty line. Overall poor mental health fell too, but not significantly. Cleanup alone did little for mental health. Greening cost about $1,597 a lot and $180 a year to maintain (South et al. 2018, abstract and secondary summaries).

**What that trial is and is not.** It is randomized, large and cheap. It is about turning blight into plain green, not about building a park, and its contact finding is self-reported use of outside space, not measured friendships.

**Green common space goes with neighbour ties.** In Chicago public housing, where residents were assigned to architecturally identical buildings, more trees and grass in common spaces went with more use of those spaces and stronger neighbour ties (Kuo et al. 1998, citation level from summaries). The assignment makes it better than most cross-sectional work, but it is one site.

**Time in nature has a dose and a plateau.** In nearly 20,000 English adults, people with 120 minutes or more of nature contact in the past week had higher odds of good health (odds ratio 1.59 at 120–179 minutes) and high wellbeing (1.23), with gains levelling off around 200–300 minutes (White et al. 2019, abstract). Cross-sectional, one week.

**Solid or weak.** Greening blight helps nearby residents: solid (one city, one trial). More green goes with more neighbour contact: moderate. Parks raise pleasure by a known amount: no.

### Libraries, community centres and makerspaces

**Libraries: valued, but the wellbeing evidence is thin.** An Arts Council England study valued library services by willingness to pay and by wellbeing analysis (Fujiwara, Lawton & Mourato 2015, citation level). The peer-reviewed version reports users would pay on average £19.51 a year in council tax to keep services beyond book lending and computers (Fujiwara, Lawton & Mourato 2019, abstract). That is a value, not a measured effect on pleasure.

**Community centres: a little evidence from place-based loneliness work.** See the loneliness strand below. Britain's Townscapes report found that towns with fewer social facilities tended to have more over-65s at risk of loneliness, and mutual aid groups formed more readily where social infrastructure was denser (Kelsey & Kenny 2021, read in part). Area correlations from a policy report.

**Makerspaces and sheds: qualitative.** UK site visits and interviews found makerspaces also work as social spaces and support wellbeing (Taylor, Hurley & Connolly 2016, abstract). Reviews of Men's Sheds find benefits to social connection and mental wellbeing reported in small descriptive and qualitative studies, with little on physical health (Wilson & Cordier 2013, abstract; Kelly et al. 2019, abstract). No trials.

**Solid or weak.** All weak. The making strand of H43 rests on plausibility and qualitative work.

### Shared meals and community kitchens

**Eating together goes with being bonded.** In a UK national survey, people who ate socially more often reported more happiness and life satisfaction, more trust, more community engagement and more friends to rely on. A path analysis suggested the direction runs from eating together to bondedness (Dunbar 2017, abstract). Cross-sectional; the path analysis cannot settle direction.

**Community kitchens: promising, under-studied.** A systematic review found 10 studies, mostly Canadian. Kitchens may improve cooking skills, social interaction and nutrition, and help with budgets and food insecurity; the authors call for more rigorous research before recommending them (Iacovou et al. 2013, abstract).

**Solid or weak.** Moderate for the association; weak for kitchens as an intervention.

### Venues, music, arts and pools

**Sharing an experience amplifies it.** In lab studies, the same chocolate tasted better when another person tasted it at the same time, and a bitter one tasted worse (Boothby, Clark & Bargh 2014, abstract). A follow-up found the effect only when the co-experiencer felt psychologically close (Boothby et al. 2016, citation level). This is the cleanest evidence for "pleasures only possible together", and it says sharing amplifies bad experiences too.

**Singing bonds strangers fast.** New adult-education classes that sang grew closer faster in the first month than craft or writing classes; by month three the others had caught up (Pearce, Launay & Dunbar 2015, abstract). Semi-naturalistic, small. For the model: shared activities speed early bonding; they do not replace time.

**Arts and culture go with satisfaction.** In UK survey data, arts, culture and sport engagement went with higher life satisfaction and happiness; sport and arts activities helped only when regular, while arts events and museum visits counted even when rare (Wheatley & Bickerton 2017, abstract). A WHO scoping review of over 900 publications found a broad role for the arts in health, across study designs from pilots to trials (Fancourt & Finn 2019, citation level). Mostly observational.

**Pools: a history of exclusion.** Wiltse's history of US public pools traces them from baths for the urban poor to mass leisure resorts to private suburban clubs, and as sites of racial conflict when swimming became mixed-sex (Wiltse 2007, citation level). No wellbeing data were found for pools as such. They enter this note as a warning on distribution.

**Solid or weak.** The amplification effect is experimental and solid in the lab. Venue-level wellbeing effects are observational.

### Loneliness interventions at place level

**Very few studies, none randomized.** A preregistered systematic review of place-based interventions for loneliness and mental health found seven studies: community facilities such as clubhouses, active use of local green space such as community gardening, and housing regeneration. Six looked promising or acceptable with little harm. Only one, an outdoor exercise park for seniors, measured a fall in loneliness, at three months, gone by nine. A Sydney regeneration study found no clear gain (Hsueh et al. 2022, read in part).

**Solid or weak.** Weak. The honest summary is that place-level loneliness interventions are untested, not that they fail.

### The commons problem

**Ostrom: commons can last, under conditions.** Against the view that shared resources are always overused (Hardin 1968, citation level), Ostrom found long-lived commons with eight features: clear boundaries, rules fitted to local conditions, users taking part in making rules, monitoring accountable to users, graduated sanctions, cheap conflict resolution, outside authorities recognizing the right to organize, and nested layers for larger systems (Ostrom 1990, citation level).

**The principles hold up.** A review of 91 studies testing them found solid empirical support, read probabilistically rather than as a checklist, with doubt about whether they scale (Cox, Arnold & Villamayor Tomás 2010, abstract). Their evidence is from forests, fisheries and irrigation, not parks or libraries.

**Urban amenities as commons is a legal argument, not a finding.** Foster and Iaione argue that city resources can be governed as commons through collaborative, polycentric arrangements (Foster & Iaione 2016, abstract). Useful as a menu of governance forms; not evidence.

**Free riding is real and punishment helps.** In repeated public-goods games, contributions decline without punishment; with costly punishment available, they rise sharply (Fehr & Gächter 2000, citation level). Replications broadly agree, with exceptions: in some subject pools punishment did not help, and counter-punishment can undo it (as summarized in the replication literature found; not read).

**Maintenance falls behind.** ASCE graded US public parks C− in 2025, up from D+, citing rising deferred maintenance alongside record visits; US National Parks alone carry more than $23 billion in deferred maintenance (ASCE 2025, secondary). The vacant-lot trial shows maintenance itself can be the intervention: mowing and trash cleanup cut shootings at least as much as greening (Moyer et al. 2019).

**Congestion: little evidence that crowding ruins urban amenities.** Searches found satisfaction studies in single parks, where perceived crowding lowered visitor satisfaction, and a review arguing crowding may weaken restoration, but no direct test. The US National Study of Neighborhood Parks, observing 174 parks in 25 cities, points the other way: many park facilities sit unused or underused (RAND summaries of Cohen and colleagues, secondary). For most urban amenities, underuse looks like the commoner failure than overuse.

**Solid or weak.** Ostrom's principles for natural commons: solid. Their transfer to amenities: untested. Free riding without sanctions: solid in the lab. Congestion as a main threat: no data; underuse is better documented.

### Distribution: who uses, and who pays in rent

**Access is unequal, more in quality than distance.** A review found minority groups in US cities often live slightly closer to parks, but have markedly less park acreage and lower park quality (Rigolon 2016, abstract). A broader review finds green space favours wealthier and whiter areas (Wolch, Byrne & Newell 2014, abstract).

**Use is unequal too.** In 2016, 44% of US adults had visited a library in the past year; 59% of college graduates had (Horrigan 2016). Income patterns were mixed. Who has time to use an amenity is itself unequal.

**New amenities can raise rents and push people out.** "Green gentrification" names the pattern: greening that improves an area draws richer residents and displaces the people it was meant for (Wolch et al. 2014; Gould & Lewis 2016, citation level, Brooklyn cases). Across new parks in ten US cities, living within half a mile of a new greenway more than tripled the odds a low-income tract gentrified, and parks near downtown raised them by about 90%; park size did not matter, against the "just green enough" claim that small parks are safer (Rigolon & Németh 2020, secondary coverage of the paper). The Atlanta Beltline study quantifies price rises near a greenway (Immergluck & Balan 2018, citation level).

**Solid or weak.** Unequal access: solid. Greenways and downtown parks raising gentrification odds: moderate, ten cities, observational.

### Amenities as exit support (kept short)

**Almost no direct evidence.** No study was found that measures whether parks, libraries or transit help someone leave a partner. Survivor accounts and service reports name transport and childcare among barriers to leaving, in small or advocacy samples (as found; not used for numbers).

**Childcare is the one public good with causal evidence on breakups, and it cuts both ways for a reader.** In Mexico, extending the school day by 3.5 hours, an implicit childcare subsidy, raised divorce rates, more so with longer exposure; the authors point to more female employment (Padilla-Romo, Peluffo & Viollaz 2022, read in part). A German study models and suggestively finds that public childcare raises divorce and single motherhood (Bauernschuster & Borck 2016, abstract).

**Women's own earnings reduce violence.** Narrowing the gender wage gap reduced domestic violence in the US, consistent with bargaining models in which an outside option protects (Aizer 2010, abstract). That is about income, not amenities, and enters only as the mechanism the economy note already uses: runway.

**The model reading, kept narrow.** A public good that lowers what it costs to live alone works as exit support through the same channel as cheap housing or a basic income. More exits are not a harm in this repo's terms; held stays are. The model has no abuse in it and makes no claim about abuse.

## 3. An amenity layer for the many-agent world (PROPOSAL)

**Everything in this section is a proposal.** Each value is ASSUMED unless a source is named, and named sources set directions or rough targets. Units follow the economy note (`R1`, one person's weekly solo rent; runway in weeks) and the friend-network design (`c`, `h`, tiers, strands, `λ`, `E_i`).

**Off by default.** One flag, `amenities`, in its own config block. With it off, nothing new is read and runs stay bit-identical, like the repo's other flags.

### Which amenities

Five public amenities and one private one. Each is set world-wide per regime, like housing.

| Amenity | Gives | Capacity per week `K` | Use cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Green** (park, greened lots) | contact chances (acquaintance encounters), cheap pleasure | effectively unlimited for 4–6 agents; set high | 1 time unit a visit, no fee |
| **Library** (rooms, books, free internet, quiet) | cheap pleasure, making capacity (rooms, tools), exit support (cuts living costs a little) | 6 seats | 1 time unit, no fee |
| **Table** (community kitchen, weekly shared meal) | contact with strand pay (fun), cheap food | 8 seats a meal, one meal a week | 1 time unit + a cleanup share |
| **Workshop** (makerspace, shed) | making capacity, contact by co-presence | 3 benches | 1 time unit + dues or rota |
| **Hall** (music, game nights, shows) | shared pleasure (amplified), fast early bonding | 1 event a week, 10 places | 1 time unit, small fee |
| **Common house** (cohousing only, housing regime D) | all of Table and Hall, members only | members | the housing note's `0.10 × R1` share |

Pools, transit and childcare are left out of the first layer. The world has no bodies to exercise, no geography to cross and no children.

### What each gives, in the model's terms

**Contact chances.** Being at an amenity in a week adds to the meeting rule. The design note's rate becomes `λ = 0.1 × start_i × (1 + housing contact + amenity contact)`, with amenity contact = Σ over amenities used of `m_a` (Green 0.3, Table 0.6, Workshop 0.4, Hall 0.6, Library 0.2; ASSUMED). People met there start as acquaintances (`c` 0.05, `h` 0), as the design note requires.

**Encounters with existing ties.** Two agents with a tie who use the same amenity in the same week get an incidental encounter: acquaintances pay the design note's 0.01 belonging term; medium and close ties get `h += 1` hour, a third of a visit (ASSUMED), and `c` moves as for a visit scaled by 1/3. No coordination cost: that is the amenity's saving. This carries Kuo et al. 1998 and Branas et al. 2018 in direction only.

**Fast early bonding.** At Hall and Table, a new tie's first 8 weeks use `g` = 0.5 instead of 0.35 (ASSUMED), so shared activity speeds early closeness. It does not raise the history ceiling `f(h)`, so close ties still take Hall's hours (Pearce et al. 2015: the gap closes by month three).

**Amplified shared pleasure.** An amenity's pleasure per use `v_a` is multiplied by `1 + α` when a tie at medium or closer uses it the same week, `α` = 0.2 (ASSUMED). The same factor applies to the amenity's bad events (Boothby et al. 2014: amplification runs both ways).

**Cheap pleasure.** Each use pays `v_a` (ASSUMED: Green 0.02, Library 0.02, Table 0.03, Workshop 0.03, Hall 0.04 in hedonic units). Green has a weekly cap at 3 visits, a plateau in the direction of White et al. 2019.

**Making capacity.** Library and Workshop let agents run making actions at lower cost: the making step's tool cost falls by half while a bench or room is held (ASSUMED). This hooks the makers code and H36's small commons of makers.

**Exit support.** Two channels, both ASSUMED in size:
1. **Runway.** A Table user's food cost falls by 10% of the economy note's `0.9 × R1` other-necessities line; a Library user's by 3%. That lengthens runway for whoever uses them, alone or paired.
2. **Ties that are one's own.** Ties formed at an amenity get `origin` = amenity. In the design note's breakup rule, such a tie follows the partner who met it there (`p_keep` 1 for that partner), not the one with more history. So amenity ties keep exit support `E_i` up through a breakup. This is the mechanism by which amenities could lower held stays, and it is testable.

### Capacity and congestion

**Over capacity, everyone gets less.** When `n` users want an amenity with capacity `K`, each gets `v_a × min(1, K / n)` and contact chances scaled the same way. Who gets a bench follows the amenity's access rule (below).

**Wear.** Each amenity has a condition stock `Q_a` from 0 to 1. Each week `Q_a −= ω_a × users`, with `ω` = 0.005 for Green and Library, 0.02 for Workshop and Table (ASSUMED). Pleasure and contact per use scale with `Q_a`. Below `Q_a` 0.3 the amenity closes until repaired.

**Underuse is a failure too.** An amenity used by fewer than 2 agents for 26 weeks loses its contact value: no one is there to meet. This carries the documented underuse of parks.

### Maintenance, and the norms that fund or protect it

**Three funding regimes, one per run** (ASSUMED shapes; the choice is author question 1):
- **M1, public levy.** A flat amenity tax inside the economy note's tax, sized to restore `Q_a` each week. No dues, open to all. This is Klinenberg's library.
- **M2, dues and rota.** Users pay dues or give time units to a rota that restores `Q_a`. Non-payers can be excluded. This is Ostrom's bounded commons, and a cohousing common house.
- **M3, voluntary.** Contributions are a choice each week; no sanction. This is the free-rider baseline.

**Protecting norms, as options agents can adopt** (H36 runs them as emergent; the first H43 run can fix them). Each maps to an Ostrom principle:
- a booking rule for Workshop and Hall (boundaries; rules fitted to the resource);
- a cleanup share at Table (provision tied to use);
- a visible contribution log (monitoring);
- warnings, then a 4-week suspension, for a non-contributor (graduated sanctions);
- a monthly meeting at a time cost (collective choice), as the housing note's cohousing governance cost.

**Domination check on norms.** A sanction is interference. Under the repo's liberty-minus-domination measure, a suspension decided by a rule users made together and can change is not domination; one imposed by one agent at will is. The amenity's access rule is recorded so the liberty audit can read it.

**Capitalization, as a sensitivity arm.** The world has no land market (housing note, section 4). To test green gentrification at all, run an arm where solo rent `R1` rises by `κ × (number of public amenities)` with `κ` in {0, 0.02, 0.05} (ASSUMED). It shows whether an amenity's gain to users survives the rent it adds to everyone.

### Fit with the housing regimes

| Housing regime | Amenity layer |
|---|---|
| A, costly solo | Amenities are the main contact source outside friend visits. The Table and Library runway cut matters most here. |
| B, cheap solo units | Amenities stand in for the space a micro-unit lacks: the space cost `s` is halved for weeks with any amenity use (ASSUMED). |
| C, shared houses | Amenities overlap with housemate contact; the expected gain is smallest. A check that the layer is not double-counting. |
| D, cohousing | The common house is a member-only Table plus Hall, funded M2. Public amenities sit on top. Compare D with the common house against B with public Table and Hall: the same services, closed against open. |

### Known-answer checks

These are checks on the world before any H43 result is read, not results.

1. **Amenities raise contact.** With the layer on, weekly encounters and new acquaintances per agent rise against the same seeds with it off (Kuo et al. 1998; Branas et al. 2018; direction only).
2. **Mostly weak ties.** Most new ties from amenities are still acquaintances after 26 weeks; a close tie still needs about 180+ hours (Hall, via friend-network design). If amenities mint close friends quickly, the layer has hard-coded friendship.
3. **Early bonding catches up.** Ties formed at Hall are closer than ties formed at Green at week 4, and not much closer at week 13 (Pearce et al. 2015).
4. **Plateau.** Pleasure from Green rises with visits and flattens past the cap (White et al. 2019).
5. **Free riding without sanctions.** Under M3, contributions fall over time and `Q_a` decays; under M2 with graduated sanctions, they hold (Fehr & Gächter 2000; Cox et al. 2010). If M3 holds up as well as M2 with no reason in the agents, the contribution choice is mis-wired.
6. **Neglect hurts.** Letting `Q_a` fall below 0.3 removes the amenity's contact and pleasure, and use drops (direction of ASCE 2025 and the cleanup arm of Moyer et al. 2019).
7. **Unequal use.** Agents with the least free time use amenities least. Report gains by wage quintile and by time budget. If every agent gains equally, the time cost is not binding (Horrigan 2016; Rigolon 2016).
8. **Capitalization can erase gains.** At the highest `κ`, the poorest agents' pleasure with everyone counted falls below the no-amenity world while users' rises (direction of Rigolon & Németh 2020; Wolch et al. 2014).
9. **Exit through runway and own ties.** The share of stay readings held falls with the layer on, through longer runway and higher `E_i`, and does not fall when both channels are switched off (Padilla-Romo et al. 2022 and Aizer 2010 for the direction of the mechanism, not this model).
10. **Off is off.** With `amenities` off, every pinned fingerprint in tests/data matches.

Checks 2, 3 and 4 are partly true by construction; they guard the code. Checks 5, 7, 8 and 9 can fail.

### Cost

Five amenities, a capacity count and a condition stock each, updated once a week. Encounters are a set intersection over at most 6 agents. No rollouts are added. The design note's bar applies: a timing probe with the flag on and off, and cuts if it adds more than 10%.

## 4. What a small model can and cannot claim

**It can show mechanisms.** For example: if ties made at an amenity follow the person who made them, then exit support rises and fewer stays are held, given these rates. Or: if contributions are voluntary, the amenity decays and its contact value goes with it. Both are statements about the model.

**It can compare regimes inside itself.** Same agents and seeds, amenities on and off, funded M1, M2 or M3, crossed with housing regimes A to D, judged on pleasure with everyone counted, on liberty minus domination, on exit support and on the held share.

**It can check directions.** The ten checks above. If a direction fails, the comparisons are suspect.

**It cannot give effect sizes for people.** The only causal sizes found are from one greening trial in one city, and they are about depression and crime, not pleasure or friendship. Every `v_a`, `m_a` and `α` is free.

**It cannot say what a city should build.** With 4–6 agents, a park and a hall are a club. Congestion, which needs crowds, will rarely bind, and underuse, which needs few users, will bind often. That is about small groups, not cities.

**It cannot settle place versus people.** Third-place and cohousing studies cannot separate the place from those who choose it, and neither can a world that assigns agents to amenities.

**It cannot say anything about gentrification beyond a sensitivity arm.** There is no land market; `κ` is a stated size, not an outcome.

**It cannot say anything about abuse.** The exit channels are runway and ties. The world has no violence.

## 5. Questions for the author

1. Should amenities be public and open (funded by levy, like a library) or bounded clubs with dues and exclusion (like a common house)? Ostrom's evidence favours boundaries; Oldenburg and Klinenberg say openness is the point. The proposal runs both; which is H43's main arm?
2. Should agents make and change amenity rules themselves (the H36 path, norms emerging), or should the first H43 run fix rules per regime and leave emergence to H36?
