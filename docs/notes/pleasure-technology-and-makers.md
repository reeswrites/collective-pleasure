## 2. Making in the notes

**Who makes pleasure.** The author's own list of pleasure goods is concrete: food, drinks, games, art, music, rooms. In the author's words, art is "creating experiences, usually by image, augmented by the other senses" (What should we be doing). And: "Art is an emotional technology and can be deployed in a number of ways, but it can be an end unto itself" (Why code?).

**Care and giving are making too.** In the author-written Relationships page, the giver role covers "entertainment, care, or assistance". Loops are co-made: "Each person is experiencing pleasure and sharing an experience that you are making together (co-creation)."

**The author is a maker by preference.** "while I do get some amount of pleasure from problem solving, I have always liked the creation side more" (Why code?). In the give/share/receive list, the author lists "Making" under pleasures not yet shared: "I like the pleasures that I like but I don't have experience sharing those. maybe can try sharing".

**How it gets made.** The author's own making rules, from the Crafting page's author-written list: "Finding, curating, collaging, and creating beauty" and "Functional and/or giftable things". The same page quotes an older note: "i def dont want to collect too many things... i want to be kinda of a minamliast or at least let it go at teh end". So the author's making is mostly flows (cooked, given, mailed), not stocks.

**Making needs tools and systems.** From "on pleasure": "set up systems: choose actor and environment and resources" and "buy good food either live near or cook it or make money to buy it and optimize it". Three routes to the same pleasure: live near a maker, make it yourself, or earn and buy.

**From scarcity to on-demand.** Pleasure On Demand opens with an 1888 utopian wish for music at home, then a line that reads as the author's: "Innovations bringing technology to the home via miniaturization, standardization, and affordability engineering." The author's outline follows: "The Bar to the Home", "The Cafe to the Home", "The Restaurant to the Home" (kegerator, espresso machine, pizza oven, ice cream maker, smoker).

**The author sees the tradeoff.** "bean to cup machines: convenience but not always the yummiest as can be, its tradeoffs". And on streaming: "having too many streaming services is bad bc you have to balance too many things and also deal with streamer slop".

**What on-demand changes (AI passage).** The pasted AI text on the same page says services like massage or a concert "cannot be stored": you store "a claim on someone's time". It also says the most on-demand products collapse Find, Supply and Serve into one object, and that this is where the trap products are. These are not the author's words.

**Safe vs extractive.** This part is the author's: "Pleasure technology has closely evolved with addiction technology... if they did, there goal would be to create safe pleasure technology, rather than extractive pleasure technology. The incentive structure is different based on the reward!" And: "Limbic capitalism is misaligned pleasure technology." And: "Harm reduction is the name of the game."

**The author's scope.** "Some people are more interested in higher impact work like reducing suffering. I am only interested in increasing pleasure. I am also interested in what can be done in the short term with mostly reconfigurations of existing technology."

**Designed goods are fine.** "For one, we can design/create food that is delicious and good for us" and "I think easy pleasure is fine, especially in the short-term, but we do have to remember that we are also focused on the long-term" (What should we be doing).

## 3. Design implications for H39

### 3a. Which agents make what

**From the author's words:** makers make food, games, art, and care (entertainment, care, assistance). Co-made loops (games, improv, sex) are made by both partners at once.

**Claude's inference:** these goods differ on three axes the model needs.
- Storable or not. Food spoils, art and games keep, care happens only in the moment. (The "cannot be stored" point comes from the AI passage, not the author.)
- Rival or not. A meal is eaten once. A game or a song can be enjoyed by the whole household.
- Solo or co-made. A loop needs two makers present; a gift needs only one.

**Claude's inference:** a first H39 world can have three maker kinds: a cook (perishable, rival), an artist or game maker (durable, shared), and a carer (time-bound service). Co-made loops can wait.

### 3b. Safe vs extractive makers

**From the author's words:** safe vs extractive pleasure technology, set by the incentive behind it; "Limbic capitalism is misaligned pleasure technology."

**Claude's inference:** give maker agents an aim. A safe maker aims at the receiver's liking. An extractive maker aims at consumption or dependence, so its goods raise wanting more than liking. This plugs into test 1 (liking vs wanting).

**Claude's inference, a candidate prediction:** if norms credit makers by volume consumed, extractive makers win credit and total liking falls. If norms credit by the receiver's later verdict, safe makers win. (The "next-day verdict" metric is from an AI passage; the author would need to endorse it.)

### 3c. How norms credit making

**From the author's words:** "I think if you have a surplus of resources then it does make sense that you have a duty of sorts to share." And: "I think it's most helpful to think that you can't complain about doing things for friends." From the earlier answers (questions.jsonl): "its the guilt and scorekeeping i dont like"; fines on a lapsed role are "allowed only by mutual choice i think".

**Claude's inference:** there are four arms worth testing.
- C0: making counts zero (today's model).
- C1: making counts as contribution by effort (hours), in the pot and in the exit split.
- C2: making counts by the pleasure it gives receivers.
- C3: no running ledger; making is a gift, and it is counted only at exit, when splitting what was built together.

**Claude's inference:** C3 is the arm closest to the author's dislike of scorekeeping. C1 and C2 need a ledger, which the author may read as scorekeeping.

### 3d. Making is itself a pleasure

**From the author's words:** "pleasure in work is pleasure over time and you get to choose or optimize for that time" (on pleasure). "I have always liked the creation side more" (Why code?). From an earlier answer: giving has an act-based part that does not need the receiver to enjoy it ("i think you can enjoy giving into a void").

**From the author's words, with a hedge:** the author's making is "for something": the Crafting page's own bullets say "Functional and/or giftable things". The "not autotelic" constraint on that page is an AI paraphrase of a 2023 note.

**Claude's inference:** a maker gets up to three payoffs: process pleasure while making, giving pleasure (act-based), and the receiver's pleasure (outcome-based). So making is not pure cost. Its net cost is effort minus process pleasure, and that differs by persona. The revised thesis (AI-drafted) calls this hedonic specialization: give each task to whoever minds it least.

**Claude's inference:** this makes crediting harder. Paying full credit for hours overpays a maker who loves the work, and paying nothing underpays one who doesn't.

### 3e. Commitment enables long projects

**From the author's words:** "I think long-term projects (goals, games, relationships) are the key to creating reward payoff structure for yourself." And: "Achieving the goal is actually less important than having one." And, on challenging projects: "Inherently probably not, but there are some properties that make them good", with a longer time horizon first on the list.

**From likely-AI text (the Commitment skeleton):** commitment converts present friction into recalled pleasure; commit to timescales ("I am not evaluating this for a year"), not behaviors.

**Claude's inference:** some making has long lead times (a game, a garden, a shared home). Under free exit, a maker may not start, because a partner can leave before the payoff. A timescale commitment norm should raise long-project making. That is testable: with and without a commitment window, how much long-lead making happens, and what happens to total pleasure.

## 4. Tensions between the notes and the current model

**Labor and care count zero.** The notes treat making as both cost and pleasure, and care as one of the things relationships make. Zero is wrong in both directions (docs/results/exit-fallbacks.md, "labor and care, which count 0").

**Pleasure is not made in the model.** Today pleasure comes from persona weights and need levels. The notes, and H39, say pleasure has to be made by someone. There are no goods for makers to make yet.

**Crediting needs a ledger, and the author dislikes scorekeeping.** Any arm that tracks contribution risks being the thing the author objects to.

**Free exit vs long projects.** The model lets partners leave. The notes say long projects carry the payoff. A maker mid-project is exposed.

**One pleasure channel.** The model cannot yet tell a safe maker from an extractive one, because it does not split wanting from liking. That waits on test 1.

**Stocks vs flows.** Slice 2 models durable items (home, pet, collection). The author's own making is mostly consumed or given away. Food and care are flows; the model has no flows of made goods.

**Home technology vs trade.** The notes celebrate bringing the bar and restaurant into the home. Claude's inference: that makes each household more self-sufficient and lowers gains from trading with makers outside it. The model has one household, so it cannot see this yet.
