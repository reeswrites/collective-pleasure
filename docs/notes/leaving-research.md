# Leaving: when and how people think about it (2026-11-14)

Wording rule: agents feel nothing. "Pleasure", "suffering", "value", "forecast" and "thinking about leaving" are glosses for modeled numbers when they refer to agents (ADR 0010).

Status: a research note, not a result. Nothing here changes code or fits a number. It answers the author's question, quoted verbatim: "maybe we can look into when and how people think about leaving? yeah idk if it's small and then grows or ppl make drastic decisions or what". The author also said people leave when they "can't find value in relationship anymore... the bad outweighs the good", not because they value leaving.

Why now: in the hybrid, agents forecast leaving only at in-future readings every 13 weeks. In the lived world 4.5% of members leave within 13 weeks of a reading; the forecasts never do (docs/results/hybrid-exit-option.md, "The second measures"). Over a year the two agree (13.4% forecast, 14.8% lived). So the forecast gets the rate about right and the timing late.

Sources were checked by web search on 2026-11-14 (title, authors, venue, and DOI or a stable URL). Read levels, as in friendship-research.md:

- "(read in part)": the full text or a detailed author summary was opened and the cited part read.
- "(abstract)": the abstract or a catalog record.
- "(secondary)": the claim comes from a press release, a review or a citing paper.
- "(citation level)": only that the work exists and what it is about.

Most sources here are at abstract or secondary level. No number below was made up; each quoted number has its read level next to it.

## In one paragraph

**Both, and the mix depends on the person and the tie.** For most people who leave, thoughts of leaving are not a single moment: they come, go and come back, often for a year or more, and satisfaction drifts down well before the end. But a large minority leave from ties that looked fine, with little visible build-up, and a decision often lands at a trigger (a fight, a move, a term ending, a new option) that sits on top of the slow drift. Leaving is seldom mutual: one partner usually starts leaving in their head long before the other knows. Thinking about leaving is common and mostly reversible: in a national sample of married people, a quarter had thought about divorce in the last six months, and a year later only about one in ten of those had separated. On forecasting, people tend to predict that a breakup will hurt more than it does; daters also predict their own relationships will last longer than outsiders do. For friends, fading is the common ending and conflict the rarer one. For housemates the evidence is thin. On whether an easy exit makes people more willing to move in (section 5): probably yes for low-stakes trying, but lock-in builds once people are in, and for marriage, cheaper exit went with fewer entries, because people use a costly exit as a commitment device. On "the bad outweighs the good" (section 6): it holds in part. Satisfaction and alternatives predict leaving, but commitment predicts better, barriers keep people in unsatisfying ties, about half of divorces come from low-distress marriages, and the good fading (disillusionment, boredom) predicts later divorce better than conflict does.

## 1. Gradual, sudden, or both?

### The strongest evidence says: mostly gradual, with fluctuation

**Satisfaction falls years before a divorce.** In the German Socio-Economic Panel, life satisfaction drops as people approach divorce, then partly recovers, and does not return fully to baseline (Lucas 2005, abstract). A press summary puts the decline at up to six years before the divorce (secondary; not checked in the paper). This is a large panel with repeated measures, so it is strong evidence that, on average, the bad builds slowly. It measures life satisfaction, not thoughts of leaving.

**Thinking about divorce is common, and it moves both ways.** Hawkins et al. (2017) surveyed about 3,000 married people aged 25 to 50 twice, a year apart (abstract). From the authors' own summary (read in part):

- 25% had thought about divorce in the last six months; 28% more had thought about it earlier but not recently.
- Of the recent thinkers, about half were "soft" thinkers (11% of the sample), about half "serious" (12%), and 2% "conflicted".
- A year later, 64% of thinkers were still thinking about it, about a quarter had stopped, and 11% had separated or divorced.
- Of non-thinkers, about 1 in 6 had started thinking about it, and 2% had separated or divorced.
- Only a third of thinkers held the same attitude a year later. Of those "done with the marriage", 36% still were, 41% had moved toward staying, and 18% had divorced.

Strength: large national sample, two waves, self-report. Two waves cannot show whether thoughts grow smoothly between them. The follow-up interviews describe "leaning-in, leaning-out, and holding-on" moving day to day over 8 to 10 months (Allen et al. 2022, abstract; qualitative, small sample).

**Thinking about leaving sits between commitment and leaving.** VanderDrift, Agnew & Wilson (2009) built a scale for "dissolution consideration" (how salient ending the relationship is while it is still intact). In a longitudinal study it mediated the link from commitment to leave behaviors, was tied to more immediate action, and explained leaving beyond commitment alone (abstract). This is the closest measured thing to "a running level of thinking about leaving" that predicts the act. Student dating samples.

**Ups and downs predict ending, beyond the average.** In two longitudinal studies of new dating relationships, people whose satisfaction fluctuated more were more likely to break up, even holding the average level constant; they also reported lower commitment (Arriaga 2001, abstract and secondary). So it is not only the running total that matters; the swings do too. One study's window was about nine weeks (secondary).

**Commitment, and what feeds it, is the best-established predictor of breakup.** In Rusbult's investment model, commitment rises with satisfaction and investments and falls with the quality of alternatives (Rusbult, Martz & Agnew 1998, citation level). A meta-analysis found the three together explain nearly two-thirds of the variance in commitment, and commitment predicts breakup (Le & Agnew 2003, abstract). A larger meta-analysis of 137 studies (37,761 people) on nonmarital breakup found relationship variables such as commitment among the best predictors, ahead of individual traits (Le et al. 2010, abstract and secondary). Strong, replicated, but mostly student dating samples. This fits the author's "the bad outweighs the good", with one addition: the model also says alternatives and sunk investments move the bar.

### Stage models: plausible shapes, weak tests

**Duck's phase model** runs from a private phase (one person weighs their dissatisfaction alone), to a dyadic phase (it is raised with the partner), a social phase (others are told), and "grave-dressing" (each builds a story of what happened). Rollie & Duck (2006) added a later "resurrection" phase (secondary, from textbook summaries; not read in the original). It is a descriptive model, widely taught, little tested as a sequence.

**Vaughan's "Uncoupling"** (1986, book; secondary) is an interview study. Its central claim: uncoupling begins with a secret. One partner (the initiator) grows unhappy, looks for alternatives and builds a life apart, often long before the other partner notices. By the time it is said aloud, the initiator has had months or years to adjust; the partner starts from zero. Rich, qualitative, not a sample anyone can generalize from.

**Kayser's marital disaffection** (1993, book; citation level) describes love fading gradually through phases in a clinical sample. We did not find a reliable summary of its specific findings, so we cite it only for the idea that disaffection is a slow process.

**Gottman's cascade.** Gottman & Levenson (1992) followed 73 couples for four years. More negativity than positivity in conflict predicted later dissolution. They proposed a cascade (unhappiness, then thinking of separation, then separation, then divorce), and said themselves it had only preliminary support (abstract and full-text excerpt). Small sample; later prediction claims from this lab are contested in the field for overfitting, which we did not check here.

**Baxter (1984)** classified disengagement trajectories, including whether the move is direct or indirect and one-sided or mutual (citation level and secondary). Our search did not confirm the "rapid vs protracted" labels sometimes attributed to it, so we do not use them.

### But a large minority leave without a visible build-up

**About half of divorces come from low-distress marriages.** In a US national panel, a cluster analysis of 509 couples who divorced between waves found about half had been in high-distress marriages and half in low-distress ones (Amato & Hohmann-Marriott 2007, abstract). People from high-distress marriages were happier after divorce; people from low-distress marriages were less happy. The authors suggest low-distress leavers had low commitment and few barriers. This is the strongest evidence that "the bad outweighs the good" is not the only path: some leave when the good is merely not enough, or when a barrier drops. It is from the US, from 1987–1994 waves, and "low distress" is measured at the earlier wave, so distress may have risen in between.

**Triggers and timing.** In the Boston Couples Study, breakups clustered at points in the school calendar (start and end of terms, holidays), and unequal involvement and mismatches in age, aims, intelligence and looks predicted breakup (Hill, Rubin & Peplau 1976, abstract). So the decision often lands at a transition, on top of an existing mismatch. "Sliding vs deciding" (Stanley, Rhoades & Markman 2006, abstract) makes the other half of the point: entering and staying are often slides, not decisions, so leaving may need a jolt to become a decision.

### Friends and housemates

**Friendships mostly fade.** In college students' accounts of ended friendships, Rose (1984) found four paths: physical separation, being replaced by new friends, growing to dislike the friend, and interference from dating or marriage; separation was the most common (abstract and secondary). Rose & Serafica (1986) found most endings were a "slow death" or loss of affection, not a fight (secondary). Recent work separates full endings from "downgrades" in closeness (secondary). Small, student, retrospective samples; consistent with friendship-research.md, which models fading as decay.

**Housemates: thin evidence.** We found no peer-reviewed longitudinal work on when unrelated adult housemates decide to move out. College roommate studies report friction over privacy, sleep schedules and mismatched expectations, and room-change requests as the first sign administrators see (single-campus studies; secondary). housing-research.md already notes self-selected survey evidence that shared renters enjoy company and fight over chores. So any housemate leaving rule would be ASSUMED.

## 2. What sets it off, and who leaves first

**Accumulated bad and a trigger are not rivals.** The panel evidence (Lucas 2005; Hawkins et al. 2017) shows a slow build in most cases; the timing evidence (Hill et al. 1976) and the low-distress divorces (Amato & Hohmann-Marriott 2007) show that the act often waits for a transition, a lowered barrier or a new alternative. The most defensible reading: a slowly moving level of dissatisfaction or doubt, with the act fired by events. How much weight each carries is not estimated anywhere we found.

**Leaving is seldom mutual.** In the Boston Couples Study, the desire to break up was seldom mutual, and unequal involvement predicted breakup (Hill et al. 1976, abstract). Sprecher (1994) is widely cited for the point that most breakups are not mutual (secondary; not checked in the paper). Vaughan (1986) describes the initiator as months or years ahead (secondary). In a US national sample, women initiated about 69% of divorces but about half of nonmarital breakups; married women reported lower relationship quality than married men (Rosenfeld 2018, secondary; only 92 divorces in the sample). Strength: the asymmetry itself is well supported; how far ahead the leaver is has no number we found.

**People stay for the partner's sake.** Over 10 weeks, daters were less likely to break up if they believed their partner was highly committed or would be highly distressed, even when their own satisfaction was low (Joel, Impett, Spielmann & MacDonald 2018, secondary). And many people hold reasons to stay and to leave at once (Joel, MacDonald & Page-Gould 2018, abstract). So the "left" partner's attachment can hold the leaver for a while.

## 3. How well do people forecast life after leaving?

**The typical error: a breakup is predicted to hurt more than it does.** People overpredicted their distress after a breakup; the error came from overestimating how bad it would be at first, not from misjudging how fast it fades (Eastwick, Finkel, Krishnamurti & Loewenstein 2008; 26 people who broke up, abstract and full text as in decision-weights-research.md). Earlier work found people neglect their own coping and overpredict how long bad feelings last (Gilbert et al. 1998, abstract). **Contested:** Levine et al. (2012) argue much of this "impact bias" is a measurement artifact; with clear questions, forecasts are fairly accurate (abstract). Safe reading: if anything, people overpredict the cost of leaving, by an unknown and disputed amount.

**But not for everyone.** People who left low-distress marriages were less happy afterwards (Amato & Hohmann-Marriott 2007), and divorce leaves average life satisfaction below its old level for years (Lucas 2005). So the error runs both ways by group: those leaving a bad tie may overpredict the pain; those leaving an "ok" tie may underpredict the loss. This split is our reading of two studies, not a tested finding.

**People also overpredict that their relationship will last.** Daters predicted their relationships would last longer than their roommates or parents did, and were less accurate; outsiders, especially roommates, predicted better (MacDonald & Ross 1999, secondary). So the forecast of staying is optimistic, and the forecast of leaving's pain pessimistic. Both push toward staying.

## 4. Thinking about leaving and coming back

**Most people who think about leaving do not leave, at least within a year.** In Hawkins et al. (2017), 89% of recent divorce thinkers were still married a year later, and of those "done with the marriage", more moved toward staying (41%) than divorced (18%) (read in part). 90% of those who had ever thought about divorce said they were glad they were still together (read in part; self-report by those who stayed).

**Many leave and come back.** On-again/off-again ("cycling") relationships are common among young adults: citing work gives about 61% having ever cycled and about 44% with their most recent partner (Dailey, Pfiester, Jin, Beck & Clark 2009, secondary); Dailey's later book puts it at about two-thirds across several samples (secondary). Cycling is linked to lower quality and more uncertainty in the same research program (secondary). Mostly young, mostly dating samples.

**Even mid-divorce, some are open to reconciling.** In couples near the end of the divorce process, a minority were open to reconciliation; the two most common reasons for divorce were "growing apart" (55%) and "not able to talk together" (53%) (Hawkins, Willoughby & Doherty 2012, abstract). "Growing apart" as the top reason supports a slow build.

## 5. Does knowing you could leave make people more willing to move in?

The question: if leaving later is easy, are people more willing to try living together? The author's answer, verbatim: "im not sure i wouldn't think so bc of sunk cost and lock in and stuff but we could research". That is treated here as a prediction to test: **an easy exit at entry does not stay easy once people are in, so it should do little to raise willingness to try, or what it raises is undone by lock-in.**

Our own model currently says the opposite. With the exit option in the forecasts, week-0 formation in B rose from 0.75 to 0.82 (docs/results/hybrid-exit-option.md): the agents value the option to leave and form more because of it. The evidence below bears on whether people do that.

### Lock-in is real: exits get harder once you are in

**Investments raise commitment, and commitment predicts staying.** In Rusbult's investment model, what has been put in (time, shared friends, shared things) raises commitment alongside satisfaction, and better alternatives lower it (Rusbult, Martz & Agnew 1998, citation level). The meta-analysis confirms investments as one of the three determinants, with commitment predicting breakup (Le & Agnew 2003, abstract). Strong and replicated. Note: in the model, investments are a rational barrier (things you would lose), not only a bias.

**Sunk cost in relationships: some evidence, hypothetical.** In two vignette experiments (951 and 275 people), participants were more likely to stay in a described relationship when money and effort had been invested; whether time also mattered differs between the paper and an earlier thesis (Rego, Arantes & Magalhães 2018, abstract and secondary). Hypothetical scenarios only. Weak-to-moderate support for a true sunk-cost bias, separate from the rational barrier above.

**Shared stuff holds couples together, for better or worse.** In a national sample of 1,184 unmarried adults, Rhoades, Stanley & Markman (2010) separated dedication from three kinds of constraint: material (a shared lease, a pet, shared things), perceived (how hard leaving would be), and felt (feeling trapped). Over eight months, more material and perceived constraint predicted staying together; more felt constraint predicted breaking up (abstract and secondary). Good sample, short window. This is the most direct evidence that moving in creates lock-in the movers did not choose.

**Sliding vs deciding.** Stanley, Rhoades & Markman (2006) argue that cohabitation raises inertia to stay regardless of fit: couples slide into a shared lease, and the constraints arrive before the decision to commit (abstract). Only about 14% name testing the relationship as their main reason to move in, and those who do report worse relationship quality (Rhoades, Stanley & Markman 2009, read in part). The inertia account is influential but debated; part of the "cohabitation effect" is selection.

**Status quo bias.** People stick with the current option more than its merits warrant, across many choice settings (Samuelson & Zeckhauser 1988, citation level). Well replicated in general; not measured for leaving a home.

### Does a trial or easy exit stay easy? Mostly no, and it may also cost satisfaction

**People prefer reversible choices but like them less.** In Gilbert & Ebert (2002), people chose changeable options over final ones, expecting no difference in how they would feel; those who could change their minds liked their choice less days later and regretted it more (read in part, via author PDF and APA summary). Lab task (choosing photos), small samples, not relationships. If it carries over, a "we can always leave" trial raises willingness to start and lowers satisfaction inside. That fits the author's doubt on staying and goes against it on entry.

**Cohabitation as a trial: early protection, later risk.** In six waves of the US National Survey of Family Growth (216,455 couple-years), premarital cohabitation went with lower divorce risk in the first year of marriage and higher risk after a few years (Rosenfeld & Roesler 2019, abstract and secondary). Contested: Manning, Smock & Kuperberg (2019) commented, and the authors replied; we did not read the comment. Observational; selection into cohabiting is not removed. It does not show that a trial "stays easy"; it shows trials do not screen out later breakups.

### Low exit costs and entry: what the divorce-law evidence says

**Easier divorce raised divorce for about a decade, then mostly reversed.** After US states moved to unilateral divorce, divorce rose for about ten years and then mostly fell back; the reform explains little of the long-run rise (Wolfers 2006, abstract). Strong design (staggered state reforms), still debated in method.

**Easier divorce went with fewer marriages, not more.** Rasul (2006) models and finds that after unilateral divorce, more people stay single; the marriages that do form may be better matched (abstract and secondary). Matouschek & Rasul (2008) test three reasons people marry against US state reforms and conclude the data fit best with marriage as a **commitment device**: people marry partly to make leaving costly (abstract). If so, lowering the cost of leaving removes part of what marriage is for, and fewer choose it. Moderate strength; economics papers on aggregate data, sensitive to model choice.

**Easier divorce lowered investment inside marriage.** Unilateral divorce reduced investment in marriage-specific capital (supporting a spouse's education, children, specialization), with home ownership depending on property rules (Stevenson 2007, abstract). So an easy exit changes behavior inside the tie, not only the decision to enter.

**Few choose a harder exit when offered.** Under Louisiana's covenant marriage (a voluntary, harder-to-leave form), only about 1 to 2% of new marriages chose it (Nock & Sanchez 2003 and later state data, secondary). Covenant couples divorced less (8.6% against 15.4% in one panel), but they were more religious and less likely to have cohabited, so selection explains much of that (secondary). This shows most people do not want a harder exit; it says little about whether an easier one draws them in.

### Which way the evidence points

**Mixed, and it splits by kind of tie.**

- **For low-stakes trying (moving in, dating), easy exit probably does raise willingness to start.** Gilbert & Ebert (2002) show people prefer reversible options; cohabitation spread as the "try first" form and most movers slide in without a decision (Stanley et al. 2006; Rhoades et al. 2009). Our model's formation rise points the same way. Moderate, indirect evidence.
- **But the exit does not stay easy once people are in.** Material constraints (lease, pets, things) build up and predict staying (Rhoades et al. 2010); investments raise commitment (Le & Agnew 2003); status quo and sunk-cost effects add a bias on top. Strong for lock-in existing; weak for its size. This supports the author's doubt about what happens after entry.
- **For high-commitment entry (marriage), lower exit costs went with fewer entries, not more.** Matouschek & Rasul (2008) and Rasul (2006). Here the author's prediction is supported, by a different mechanism than sunk cost: people use costly exit as a commitment device, so they value the barrier.
- **Net:** the author's prediction ("I wouldn't think so") is supported for commitment-device ties and for life after entry, and not clearly supported for low-stakes trying, where reversibility probably does draw people in. No study we found tests willingness to start cohabiting or house-sharing against an experimentally varied exit cost. That is the gap.

**What would test it in the model (options).** (i) Let shared things accumulate as a material constraint that raises the cost of leaving week by week, so the exit priced at entry is cheaper than the exit at week 26; then see whether formation still rises with the exit option. Needs an ASSUMED accumulation rate. (ii) An arm where the agent forecasts with the exit cost it will face later, not the one it faces now. (iii) A satisfaction penalty for reversible arrangements (Gilbert & Ebert 2002) would be a new ASSUMED weight; lean no, since one lab study on photos is too thin.

## 6. Is leaving "the bad outweighs the good, compared with outside"?

The account to test: people leave when they can no longer find value in the relationship; the bad outweighs the good, against what is outside. The author agreed with this, with a hedge, verbatim: "yeah i tbjnk that makes sense to me but maybe we should research that too". It is close to what the hybrid's leave rule already does (stay value against outside option). Below is where the evidence supports it and where it breaks.

### Where it holds

**Satisfaction and alternatives both matter, as the account says.** In the investment-model meta-analysis (52 studies, about 11,600 people), satisfaction, quality of alternatives and investments together explained nearly two-thirds of the variance in commitment, and commitment predicted staying (Le & Agnew 2003, abstract; the commitment–persistence correlation of about .47 is from a citing paper, secondary). Satisfaction is the "good minus bad"; alternatives are the "compared with outside". Strong, replicated.

**Divorced people from bad marriages do better after.** Those leaving high-distress marriages became happier (Amato & Hohmann-Marriott 2007, abstract). For them, the account works as a forecast too.

### Where it breaks

**1. Satisfaction alone is not the best predictor; commitment is.** In the breakup meta-analysis (137 studies, 37,761 people), commitment was among the strongest predictors of nonmarital breakup (Le et al. 2010; secondary, we could not read its effect-size table). Commitment adds investments and alternatives to satisfaction, and dissolution consideration adds more beyond commitment (VanderDrift et al. 2009). So "net good" predicts leaving, but less well than a measure that also counts what you would lose and where you would go. The hybrid's rule already compares to an outside option; it has no investments term.

**2. People stay in unsatisfying ties because of barriers.** Levinger's (1965, 1976) model has three forces: attractions (the good inside), alternatives (the good outside) and barriers (costs of leaving: money, children, religion, shared home, what others think) (citation level and secondary). Barriers keep people in ties with low attraction. Evidence: material and perceived constraints predict staying over eight months, net of dedication (Rhoades, Stanley & Markman 2010, abstract); people stay for a partner they believe is committed or would be hurt, even when their own satisfaction is low (Joel et al. 2018, secondary); housing and money keep people in abusive relationships (housing-research.md). Strong that barriers matter; weak on how much.

**3. People leave satisfying-enough ties.** About half of divorces in a US panel came from low-distress marriages, and those who left them were less happy after (Amato & Hohmann-Marriott 2007, abstract). The account would predict they stay. Possible reasons the authors give: low commitment, few barriers. A better alternative could also explain it, which the account allows; low barriers it does not, unless barriers are counted.

**4. The bad may weigh more than the good, but the famous ratio is weak.** In general, bad events have stronger and longer effects than equal good ones (Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Finkenauer & Vohs 2001, citation level; broad review, widely accepted). Gottman's lab reported that stable couples showed about 5 positive to 1 negative interactions during conflict, unstable ones about 0.8 to 1, and that negative, not positive, interaction separated distressed couples (Gottman 1994, book, citation level; figures as restated in a later paper from the lab, secondary). How strong: the 5:1 number comes from small samples in one lab. Heyman & Slep (2001) showed that divorce-prediction models without cross-validation, as in that lab's work, overstate accuracy badly (abstract and PMC full text). We found no independent replication of 5:1 as a threshold. Read: negativity bias, yes in general; 5:1 as a law, no.

**5. The absence of good often matters more than the presence of bad.** This is the biggest break with "the bad outweighs the good".

- In the PAIR project (168 couples followed 13 years), early conflict and negativity did little to predict divorce; disillusionment did, meaning falling love, less affection and more ambivalence (Huston, Caughlin, Houts, Smith & George 2001, abstract). Moderate sample, long follow-up.
- Over 14 years, couples who divorced early showed volatility and negative emotion; couples who divorced later showed coolness and little emotion, the absence of the good (Gottman & Levenson 2000, abstract and press release; 79 couples; same cross-validation caveat).
- Boredom seven years into marriage predicted lower satisfaction nine years later, beyond conflict (Tsapelas, Aron & Orbuch 2009, secondary).
- "Growing apart" (55%) and "not able to talk together" (53%) were the most common reasons for divorce (Hawkins et al. 2012, abstract).

So two paths: an early, loud one (bad present) and a later, quiet one (good fading). Moderate evidence, mostly US marriages. The author's own words, "can't find value in relationship anymore", fit the quiet path well; "the bad outweighs the good" fits the loud one.

### What it would mean for the leave rule (options)

- **H. Count barriers separately from value (lean: yes, as a check first).** The hybrid already compares stay value to an outside option, and exit costs exist in the fallbacks. Check whether a member can be held by barriers with low stay value (the "held" lens does this). If it can, the model already covers Levinger's barriers; no new rule.
- **I. Let the good fading count, not only suffering (lean: yes, check first).** In `pleasure − w × suffering`, a fall in pleasure already lowers the score, so the quiet path may already be there. Measure: among lived exits, what share came with rising suffering and what share with falling pleasure at flat suffering? If the quiet path never happens, that is a gap.
- **J. Negativity weighting (lean: no new rule).** `w` already weighs suffering against pleasure per persona (decision-weights-research.md). Using 5:1 as a weight is not supported.
- **K. An investments term (lean: maybe, later).** Time together and shared things raising the stay score would match the investment model and section 5's lock-in. It is one more ASSUMED rate, and it overlaps with option (i) in section 5; do one or the other, not both.

## 7. What this could mean for the model (options, not decisions)

Each item is a lean for the author to confirm or reject. Each number named is not set here; where it would come from is said. No parameter is to be fitted to an outcome of these runs.

**A. A running tally that triggers a reading (lean: yes, as an arm).** Instead of in-future readings only at fixed 13-week marks, each agent keeps a running, decaying sum of weekly net value (pleasure − w × suffering, against its outside option). When the sum crosses a threshold, it takes a reading at once, in the lived world and inside forecasts. Grounds: dissolution consideration as a measured step between commitment and leaving (VanderDrift et al. 2009); slow pre-divorce decline (Lucas 2005). Matches the author's "the bad outweighs the good" as a tally, not a valuation of leaving. Numbers needed: the decay rate (how fast old weeks stop counting) and the threshold. Neither has a source that maps to our units; both would be ASSUMED, set before any run, and swept as sensitivity arms. Check it should pass: forecasts then leave within 13 weeks at some rate; whether that rate matches the lived 4.5% is a second measure, not a target to tune.

**B. Fluctuation counts too (lean: maybe, later).** Arriaga (2001) says swings predict ending beyond the average. A tally that also tracks variance would add one more ASSUMED number. Only worth it if A alone leaves timing still late.

**C. Thinking is reversible (lean: yes, it comes free with A).** A decaying tally falls back when good weeks return, which gives "thought about it, came back" without a new rule. Hawkins et al. (2017) gives a human shape to compare against (a quarter of thinkers stop within a year, about one in ten leave), as a story check, not a fit: our week is not a survey wave, and "thinking" in the model is a number we define.

**D. Trigger events (lean: yes, small).** Readings also fire at events that already exist in the world: a household change, a money shock, a friend tie gained or lost, a lease or trial end. Grounds: breakups cluster at transitions (Hill et al. 1976); low-distress divorces suggest barriers dropping matter (Amato & Hohmann-Marriott 2007). Needs no new number if it only adds a reading; adding a jump to the tally at an event would need an ASSUMED size.

**E. Leaver/left asymmetry (lean: yes, it may already be there; check first).** Each member has its own tally, so one can cross long before the other. Worth a measure before any rule: in lived runs, how many weeks before an exit did the leaver's tally turn negative, and was the other's? If the asymmetry is already produced, do nothing. A "staying for the partner" term (Joel et al. 2018) would be a new weight, ASSUMED, and should wait.

**F. Forecast-accuracy arm (lean: as a sensitivity arm only).** Today forecasts are rollouts with no human bias (decision-weights-research.md, question 1). An arm could inflate forecast suffering after leaving (Eastwick et al. 2008) and inflate the forecast value of staying (MacDonald & Ross 1999). Both push toward staying. The size is disputed (Levine et al. 2012) and no source gives it in our units; it would be ASSUMED and run as a range, never tuned to match lived exits.

**G. Friends fade, housemates unknown (lean: no new rule now).** Friend ties already decay (friendship-research.md). Housemate leaving has no evidence base we found; any rule there should be labeled ASSUMED in the many-agent spec.

## 8. What this can and cannot justify

**It can justify** treating leaving as a slowly moving level with event-timed acts, letting each member's level move separately, and letting thoughts of leaving fall back. **It cannot justify** any decay rate, threshold, trigger size or bias size; nor carrying survey percentages over to agent weeks. It says nothing about these agents' decisions as evidence about people.

**The weakest links.** Most sources read at abstract or secondary level. Stage models (Duck, Vaughan, Kayser) are descriptive and little tested. The breakup-forecasting study has 26 people, and the bias is contested. Dating samples are mostly students. Housemate evidence is nearly absent. Sprecher (1994), Rosenfeld (2018), Dailey et al. (2009) and MacDonald & Ross (1999) figures come from citing work, not the papers.

## 9. Questions for the author

1. Option A: should the trigger be the tally crossing a threshold (a level), or the tally falling fast (a slope), or both?
2. Should the same tally drive forecasts (in-future readings) and the lived world, so the two can only differ by what they forecast, not by when they look?
3. Option F: should agents carry the human tendency to overpredict the pain of leaving, or stay accurate forecasters with this as a sensitivity arm only?
4. Section 5: should the exit cost grow with time together (shared things as lock-in), so that the exit an agent prices at formation is cheaper than the one it later faces?
5. Section 6: before any change to the leave rule, measure the two paths in lived exits (suffering rising vs pleasure fading at flat suffering)? If the quiet path never appears, is that a gap to fix?
