## 0. Why debt, and why here

**The world has no borrowing yet.** H40 compares a calibrated market world with a funded basic income. Its design says: "No borrowing: an unpaid bill is a missed bill, which adds suffering" (docs/notes/economy-calibration.md, item 7). Credit is listed under "not modeled, on purpose".

**Four results already point at debt.** Poorer partners concede every contested exit (docs/results/exit-fallbacks.md). A running tally between partners lowered pleasure a little, while crediting only at exit did nothing (docs/results/h39-makers.md); under the rerun the tally's harm is "fragile, not gone" (docs/results/h39b-makers-rerun.md). Owning a home locks couples in (docs/notes/housing-research.md, Farnham et al. 2011). And the author dislikes guilt and scorekeeping in relationships, but is fine with reliance.

**Debt is all four at once.** It is a running tally. It is a claim that survives exit. It can hold people in place. And it is the money version of the extractive maker: a party whose gain is another's loss over time.

## 1. What each tradition claims

### Graeber: debt as a moral relation that power turns into a weapon

**Debt is older than coins and is a moral relation first.** Graeber argues credit systems came before coinage and barter was rare inside communities (Graeber 2011). The point for this repo is not the dating but the claim that debt turns a relation between people into a sum, and that sums can be enforced by violence.

**Three moralities run through all economies.** "Baseline communism" (from each according to ability, to each according to need), "exchange" (equal parties, accounts kept, settled), and "hierarchy" (unequal parties, governed by custom). Graeber calls baseline communism the foundation of all sociability, and offers a test: in it, no accounts are taken (as quoted in Kay 2012 and summarized in Schmid's review; summary level).

**Exchange is what makes walking away possible.** On Graeber's account, paying off a debt is what lets two parties call it even and part. Debt is an exchange not yet finished, so it keeps people tied. This is a paraphrase of the book from secondary summaries; it is the reading that matters most here.

**Human economies use money to rearrange relations; commercial economies use it to buy things.** In a human economy, money (bridewealth, blood-money) marks that a person cannot really be paid for. Graeber argues markets became impersonal when states, armies and coinage made social life calculable.

**Debt cancellation is old and was routine.** Rulers periodically cancelled debts to stop unrest. Graeber's own proposal is a jubilee for both sovereign and consumer debt.

**Steelman.** Debt is not neutral plumbing. It carries a morality ("you must pay what you owe") that makes the debtor feel guilty and makes coercion look just. Pleasure falls because guilt and fear enter relations; freedom falls because the creditor holds a claim that follows you out the door.

**Critics.** Historians and economists dispute parts of the history: DeLong alleged factual mistakes, Selgin argued Graeber misread Adam Smith, and Beggs found the anecdotes did not add up to an explanation. Mason defended the book as a complement to heterodox economics (summarized from the Wikipedia article's reception section; secondary). The three moralities are a framework, not a finding. Nobody has measured them.

### Hudson: clean slates as anti-oligarchy policy

**Bronze Age rulers cancelled debts to keep creditors from taking over.** Hudson argues that Near Eastern kings proclaimed clean slates on accession or after wars, freeing debt-bondsmen and returning land. The aim was to stop an oligarchy of creditors from owning the population (Hudson 2018; summary level from the author's site and reviews).

**Interest compounds faster than economies grow, so debt must be written down sometime.** The question is only who loses: creditors in a jubilee, or debtors in foreclosure.

**Hudson and Goodhart (2018) concede that a literal jubilee is hard today**, because most lending runs through banks and insurers, not palaces. They argue the aim (keeping debt within what people can repay) could be met by other means, such as equity-style student loans and land taxes (abstract level).

**Steelman.** A society that never writes debt down drifts toward a creditor class and a held class. That is domination in the repo's exact sense: one party can make the other's life worse at will, and the other cannot leave.

**Critics.** I did not find or check scholarly critics of Hudson's Bronze Age readings in this session. Treat the history as his reconstruction.

### Lazzarato: debt governs the self

**The creditor–debtor relation is the basic social relation of neoliberalism.** Lazzarato argues debt makes people manage themselves as small firms, promising future behaviour in exchange for present money. The debtor polices their own choices so as to stay creditworthy (Lazzarato 2012; summary level).

**Steelman.** This is a freedom claim, not a pleasure claim. A debtor may be no poorer, but their future choices are pre-committed. They pick the safer job, the steadier partner, the quieter life.

**Critics.** A review notes the genealogy rests on theory, not empirical data (Canadian Journal of Sociology review; summary level). The claim is hard to test as stated.

**It has a measurable core.** Student debt pushed graduates toward higher-paid jobs and away from low-paid public-interest work, in a natural experiment where a university swapped loans for grants (Rothstein & Rouse 2011, abstract level). That is "debt shapes the self" in a form a model can carry: debt changes which options a person takes.

### The Debt Collective and Strike Debt: debtors as a class that can bargain

**Debtors are many and scattered; organized, they have leverage.** A debtors' union treats a refusal to pay as a strike. The case is made in *Can't Pay, Won't Pay* (Debt Collective 2020; summary level).

**Rolling Jubilee showed debt is cheap to buy.** From November 2012, Strike Debt bought defaulted medical debt for pennies on the dollar and cancelled it. Reports put the totals near $15 million of debt abolished by 2014, with sources differing (Strike Debt, Wikipedia and press; secondary).

**One strike won.** The "Corinthian 15" refused to pay federal loans from a defrauded for-profit chain and invoked borrower defense. In June 2022 the Education Department discharged about $5.8 billion for some 560,000 former Corinthian students (NPR 2022; press).

**Steelman.** Debt is a power relation, and the remedy for a power relation is countervailing power. This is exit's twin: voice, done collectively.

**Critics.** One large win is a case, not an evaluation. And Rolling Jubilee's own method, buying old debt cheaply, is exactly what Kluender et al. (2025) tested at scale, with null results (section 2).

### Marx: credit and fictitious capital

**Credit lets capital claim income that does not yet exist.** In *Capital* Volume III, Part V, Marx calls interest-bearing claims such as bonds and shares "fictitious capital": titles to future surplus that trade as if they were wealth (Marx 1894; standard text, summary level). The left reading: credit speeds accumulation and makes crises worse when the claims cannot be met.

### Feminist critiques: debt lands on social reproduction

**Microcredit turned women's networks into collection systems.** Federici (2014) argues microfinance and household debt made debt a main means of accumulation and enlisted women's solidarity groups to enforce repayment (summary level).

**Brazil financialized its welfare state.** Lavinas (2017) argues social benefits became collateral for consumer loans, so inclusion meant debt rather than rights (summary level).

**Debt is lived in households and bodies.** Cavallero and Gago (2021) trace how debt in Argentina falls on women and on domestic work, and frame refusing to hide it as politics (summary level).

**Steelman.** Who carries debt inside a household is not neutral. The person doing care absorbs the shortfall, in time and stress.

**A complication from the same literature.** In Nairobi, mostly married women with their own income joined ROSCAs, and the authors read it as a way to protect savings from a husband's claims (Anderson & Baland 2002, abstract level). Informal credit can be a tool of the weaker partner, not only a burden on her.

### Non-market and alternative credit

**Mutual credit.** Members trade on account; balances sum to zero; no interest, or little. The Swiss WIR network (since 1934) is the longest-running case. Sardex in Sardinia is a recent one. LETS are small local versions.

**Time banks.** An hour of anyone's help earns one hour of credit. Equal hours, not equal wages.

**ROSCAs.** A group pays into a pot each round; one member takes the pot; it rotates. Each member is a saver early and a borrower late, or the reverse, with no interest and no outside lender (Besley, Coate & Loury 1993).

**Interest-free lending.** Charitable no-interest loans, such as Australia's NILS, for essentials.

**Islamic finance (not a left tradition).** Riba, interest, is prohibited; finance is built from sale, lease and partnership contracts instead. El-Gamal (2006) argues much of it replicates interest in legal form ("Shari'a arbitrage"), so the prohibition often changes the paperwork more than the economics (summary level).

**Steelman, all together.** Credit is useful; interest and an outside creditor class are the problem. Keep the smoothing, drop the extraction.

### The counterpoints

**Credit is freedom across time.** Mainstream economics treats borrowing as consumption smoothing: a bad month need not mean hunger or a missed bill. A person with credit has more options, not fewer. On this view, cutting people off from credit takes away a choice and pushes them toward worse ones.

**Debt relief has costs.** If debts are expected to be forgiven, people may stop paying, and lenders may stop lending to the very people relief was meant to help.

**Debt and distress are correlated, but correlation is not cause.** People fall into debt because something already went wrong: illness, job loss, divorce. Relieving the debt may not undo the cause.

## 2. What the evidence says

Labels: **solid** means a randomized or strong natural experiment, published in a refereed journal, read at least at abstract level. **Mixed** means solid studies point different ways. **Weak** means correlational, self-reported, sponsor-commissioned, or a single case.

### Access to credit

**Solid: high-cost consumer credit can help some marginal borrowers.** A South African lender randomly approved marginal applicants for loans at about 200% APR. Borrowers gained in employment and reduced hunger and poverty, and the authors read this as welfare-improving, with caution (Karlan & Zinman 2010).

**Solid: microcredit is modestly positive, not transformative.** Six randomized evaluations across six countries found "modestly positive, but not transformative" effects (Banerjee, Karlan & Zinman 2015). A pooled Bayesian analysis of seven experiments found effects on business and consumption "unlikely to be transformative and may be negligible" (Meager 2019). In the Philippines, microloans slightly lowered subjective well-being while improving risk coping and ties (Karlan & Zinman 2011).

**Mixed: payday credit.** Access raised difficulty paying rent, mortgage and utilities, and delayed medical care, in a border design (Melzer 2011). In the UK, payday loans gave short-lived liquidity gains, then more borrowing, defaults and overdraft breaches (Gathergood, Guttman-Kenney & Hunt 2019). But after natural disasters, access to payday lenders offset part of the rise in foreclosures (Morse 2011). And near the approval cutoff, payday loans had no detectable effect on credit scores either way (Bhutta, Skiba & Tobacman 2015).

**Mixed: payday bans.** After Oregon capped rates, borrowing fell, and former borrowers moved partly into bank overdrafts and late bill payment (Zinman 2010). The survey was funded by a payday-lender foundation, and a critic argued the gap reflects the recession, not the cap (Oron 2009, working paper). Bank overdraft fee income rose after states banned payday lending (Morgan, Strain & Seblani 2012).

**The reading.** Credit helps against a one-off shock and hurts when it becomes a way to live with a permanent shortfall. A ban removes both the help and the harm, and people fall back on the next-worst option.

### Debt relief

**Solid: relieving old, already-written-off debt does almost nothing.** Two RCTs forgave $169 million of medical debt for 83,401 people, debt sold to collectors. They found no effect on financial distress, mental or physical health, or financial wellness on average, some harm in pre-registered subgroups, and fewer payments on other medical bills (Kluender, Mahoney, Wong & Yin 2025). This is the Rolling Jubilee method, tested.

**Solid: relieving debt that still binds helps.** Chapter 13 bankruptcy protection, identified by random judge assignment, raised earnings and lowered five-year mortality and foreclosure (Dobbie & Song 2015). In an experiment on distressed credit card borrowers, delayed write-downs of principal improved financial and labor outcomes; lower monthly payments did not (Dobbie & Song 2020).

**Moderately solid: student debt relief raised mobility and income.** Borrowers whose private student loans were discharged because the creditor could not prove title moved more, changed jobs more, and earned about $3,000 more over three years (Di Maggio, Kalda & Yao 2019). This is a working paper; I found no journal version. Teachers value loan forgiveness, but red tape keeps many eligible ones from claiming it (Jacob, Jones & Keys 2024).

**Solid: a mass, political bailout had costs.** India's 2008 waiver for over 60 million rural households led to more defaults, defaults tied to the electoral cycle, and no gains in productivity, wages or consumption (Giné & Kanz 2018).

**The reading.** Write-downs work when the debt is still shaping what people do. They do little when the debt is already dead. Relief that people learn to expect changes repayment.

### Debt and wellbeing

**Strong correlation, cause unknown.** A meta-analysis of 65 papers found debt strongly associated with mental disorder (OR 3.24), depression (OR 2.77) and suicide (Richardson, Elliott & Roberts 2013). The authors say causality is hard to establish. In US young adults, higher debt relative to assets went with stress, depression and higher diastolic blood pressure, after controls (Sweet et al. 2013).

**Weak but relevant: consumer debt and couples.** Consumer debt directly predicted later marital conflict in a national longitudinal sample (Dew 2007). Correlational.

**The tension.** Debt and misery go together very strongly, but the best RCT of relief found no mental health effect. Either the debt that was relieved was not the debt doing harm, or debt marks distress more than it causes it. Kluender et al. read it as the latter for their case.

### Alternatives

**Moderately solid: WIR mutual credit is countercyclical.** Firms use WIR credit more when cash is scarce in a recession, so WIR moves against GDP (Stodder 2009).

**Weak: Sardex.** Firms in transaction cycles had higher turnover and smaller balance build-ups (Iosifidis et al. 2018, abstract level). Correlational, and a co-author works for Sardex.

**Weak: LETS.** LETS help employability only indirectly; they work best as seedbeds for self-employment and exchange outside work (Williams et al. 2001).

**Weak: time banks.** A systematic review found consistent positive signals on health and social capital, but low study quality and no cost data (Lee et al. 2020).

**Solid theory, specific evidence: ROSCAs.** They let people without access to credit buy lumpy goods sooner than saving alone (Besley, Coate & Loury 1993, theory). They protect women's savings inside the household (Anderson & Baland 2002).

**Weak: no-interest loans.** NILS evaluations report better wellbeing for most clients, but they are self-reported and commissioned by the program's sponsors (Centre for Social Impact 2014).

**Moderately solid but confounded: Islamic loans default less.** In Pakistan, Islamic loans defaulted at less than half the rate of conventional ones, and less during Ramadan (Baele, Farooq & Ongena 2014). The authors attribute part of this to religion, not to the absence of interest, so it says little about interest as such.

### Bottom line on "a good world or not"

**Solid:** credit helps with one-off shocks; payday credit as a way of life hurts; bans push people to the next-worst option; write-downs of binding debt help; relief of dead debt does nothing; expected bailouts change repayment.

**Not supported by evidence:** that debt as such causes most of the misery it travels with; that ending interest is enough; that the three moralities or the "indebted subject" exist as measurable things; that jubilees worked as the left histories say (the history is contested and was not checked here).

**Untested at scale:** mutual credit for households, debtors' unions, gift norms as a replacement for credit.

## 3. Connections to this repo

**Baseline communism is the author's preference, named.** "Fine with reliance, against scorekeeping" is Graeber's test: no accounts taken. The pattern atlas found 93% of giving is reliance, bill-paying into the gap (docs/results/pattern-atlas.md). That is already baseline communism inside the household.

**Gift-at-exit vs a running tally is communism vs exchange, almost exactly.** In H39, crediting making only at exit did nothing (G − Z −0.0004); the running tally's pull on behaviour cost pleasure (BP − G −0.0045). In H39b the tally's harm became "fragile, not gone", and households barely formed under the recalibrated agents. Graeber's frame predicts the direction: a live tally turns a shared life into exchange, and exchange invites people to act for the account.

**But Graeber also says the tally is what lets people leave.** Settling accounts is how two parties part as equals. Baseline communism works where people expect to stay. This is a real tension for the repo, because exit power is central: a norm with no accounts may be pleasant while together and leave the poorer partner with nothing at exit. H39's gift-at-exit rule is the repo's answer so far: no running tally, a settlement only at the door.

**Debt is exit lock-in, like an owned home.** Owning a home held couples together in down markets (Farnham et al. 2011, via the housing note). Joint debt works the same way, and worse: a home can be sold, a debt can only be paid or split. Survivors report partners damaging their credit, which blocked housing and loans (Hess & Del Rosario 2018, via the housing note). In the world, debt would shorten runway, the measure of exit power. A household is "held" when the poorer partner's runway is under `k` weeks; debt makes runway negative.

**Credit can also extend runway.** If an agent can borrow to cover the first months alone, credit is an exit fund. Whether debt lengthens or shortens runway depends on terms and on whether the shortfall is temporary. That is exactly the shock-vs-way-of-life split in the evidence.

**Extractive lenders are the money version of extractive makers.** H39 had extractive makers: their making gave them pleasure and cost the partner. An interest-charging lender with an income limit is the same shape in money: its gain is the borrower's ongoing loss. The H39 lesson carries over: what costs pleasure is not the transfer itself but the running claim's pull on behaviour.

**Poorer partners concede, and debt would sharpen that.** In exit-fallbacks, every contested exit was conceded by the partner who ran short. Debt is a mechanism for running short.

## 4. Proposed debt regimes for H40 (PROPOSAL)

**Prerequisite: agents must plan beyond one week.** Borrowing trades this week against later weeks, so its whole value lives past one week. The recalibrated agents see one week: paying a shared bill is worth +0.005 to the payer over one week and +1.01 over 52 (docs/results/agent-recalibration.md). One-week agents would borrow whenever it helps today and never value repaying. No debt arm should run until the bill horizon (v2b) or a longer planner exists and passes its own check.

**Shared measures.** Pleasure with everyone counted; runway and the share of "held" households (economy note, item 9); debt-to-income; share in arrears; debt carried out of an exit; and, for the tally question, how often agents act for the account (extra work or making blocks above a no-account baseline).

**Shared rule on exit.** Joint debt is split at exit by the world's exit rule (F0–F5). Each arm reports how much debt a leaver carries out.

### D0. No borrowing (today)

- **Parameters:** none; as in the H40 market design.
- **Known-answer checks:** reproduces the H40 market arm exactly (same seeds, same rows); a missed bill adds suffering as under `bills_hurt`.

### D1. Market credit with interest and income-based limits

- **Parameters:** weekly interest `r` (ASSUMED: 20% a year as the default; a payday-like 300% a year as a sensitivity arm); credit limit `L = m × weekly income` (ASSUMED `m = 4`); minimum payment `p` of the balance each week (ASSUMED 2%).
- **Known-answer checks:**
  1. With `r = 0` and a shock that reverses (a job loss that ends), pleasure is at least D0's: smoothing works (direction of Karlan & Zinman 2010; Morse 2011).
  2. With `r` at the payday level and a permanent shortfall, debt-to-income ratchets up and arrears rise over time (direction of Melzer 2011; Gathergood et al. 2019).
  3. Removing D1 after agents rely on it raises missed bills, not hardship-free living (direction of Zinman 2010).
  4. With `m = 0`, identical to D0.

### D2. Market credit plus periodic jubilee

- **Parameters:** D1's, plus a jubilee period `J` weeks (ASSUMED 7 years, 364 weeks; sensitivity 2 years); whether agents anticipate it (yes/no).
- **Known-answer checks:**
  1. With `J` longer than the run, identical to D1.
  2. Cancelling debt that an agent was already not paying (in arrears past a cutoff) leaves pleasure unchanged (direction of Kluender et al. 2025).
  3. Cancelling debt that was still being serviced raises the debtor's later pleasure and runway (direction of Dobbie & Song 2015, 2020).
  4. With anticipation on, repayment falls as the jubilee nears (direction of Giné & Kanz 2018).
- **Note:** there are no lenders as agents, so the supply response (credit drying up before a jubilee) must be a rule: the limit `L` shrinks linearly over the last `J/4` weeks (ASSUMED). This is a stand-in, not a finding.

### D3. Zero-interest mutual credit or a time bank

- **Parameters:** each member's balance may go as low as `−L` (ASSUMED `L = 4` weeks of the median bill); no interest; unit is money (mutual credit) or a time unit (time bank: one unit of help earns one credit, whoever gives it).
- **Known-answer checks:**
  1. Balances across members sum to zero every week (conservation; a test, not an experiment).
  2. Use rises when money is scarce, e.g. during job loss spells (direction of Stodder 2009).
  3. With `L = 0`, identical to D0.
  4. In the time-bank version, an hour of care and an hour of making earn the same credit, whatever the agents' wages.

### D4. A debtors' union

- **Parameters:** D1's, plus a membership threshold `q` (share of indebted agents who must join; ASSUMED 0.3) and a concession `c` won when the threshold is met (ASSUMED: interest cut to `r/2`, or a write-down of 20% of principal). The lender is a scripted counterpart, so the concession is a rule.
- **Known-answer checks:**
  1. With `q > 1` (the union never forms), identical to D1.
  2. Concessions occur only in weeks when membership is at least `q`.
  3. With `c` set as a write-down, results move toward D2's serviced-debt case (check 3 there).
- **Note:** the evidence here is one case (Corinthian). The arm tests the mechanism, not the movement.

### D5. A gift or baseline-communism norm among friends

- **Parameters:** friends cover each other's shortfalls with no record and no repayment; the giver gives only from savings above their own `g` weeks of runway (ASSUMED `g = 8`, the held threshold); radius is the existing friend graph.
- **Known-answer checks:**
  1. No ledger exists, so there is no account to act for: the "act for the account" measure equals D0's (the gift-at-exit result of H39, by construction).
  2. With the radius set to the household only, identical to today's pot giving.
  3. Report persistent one-way flows (the same giver covering the same receiver for a year). Graeber notes communism can slide into hierarchy; this measures whether it does.
  4. Exit leaves no debt, so runway at exit is never negative from this channel.

### Suggested order

D0 vs D1 vs D5 first: no credit, market credit, and the author's own preference. D2, D3 and D4 after, each as a variation on D1.

## 5. What a small model can and cannot claim

**It can compare regimes inside one world.** Under stated assumptions, it can say which regime gives more pleasure with everyone counted, and which leaves fewer held households. It can show when credit extends runway and when it shortens it.

**It can check itself against known directions.** The checks in section 4. A regime that misses its known direction should not be read.

**It can sharpen the tally finding.** D1 and D5 are the money version of H39's tally vs gift-at-exit. If the tally's pull shows up with money too, that is stronger than either alone.

**It cannot say what debt does to real people.** The evidence above does that, imperfectly. The model has no lenders who adjust supply, no prices, no general equilibrium, no legal system, no credit scores.

**It cannot test Graeber's history, Hudson's Bronze Age, or Lazzarato's subject.** Agents feel nothing and have no selves to govern. "Governance of the self" can only appear as debt changing which actions agents choose, as in Rothstein & Rouse.

**It cannot say whether debtors' unions work.** It can only say what a concession would do if won.

**Its rates and limits are ASSUMED.** The world's units are abstract, and every number in section 4 is a guess to be varied.

## 6. Questions for the author

1. Which debt do you mean first: debt between people who know each other (partners, friends: Graeber's tally), or debt to an outside lender (Hudson, the Debt Collective)? They test different things, and the first connects straight to H39.
2. If market credit raised pleasure with everyone counted but also raised the share of held households, would you call that a good world or not? The answer decides which measure leads.
3. Should D5 (no accounts among friends) settle anything at exit, like H39's gift-at-exit, or truly nothing? Graeber's account suggests no settlement keeps people tied; the repo's exit rule suggests a settlement protects the poorer partner.
