Liking vs wanting: how much gap each environment and rule allows

ADR 0018 test 1. Scripted agents, exact solves, 80 personas drawn from an assumed parameter box (seed 20261110), 28 days of 4 slots. Agents feel nothing: "liking" and "wanting" are glosses for modeled values. Every parameter is assumed. Prereg: docs/results/liking-wanting-prereg.md. Results: docs/results/liking-wanting.md.

What was asked

Pleasure is split in two. Liking is the payoff that counts. Wanting is what drives choice for an agent that acts on it: liking plus a pull from cues that grows with use (sensitization). A loop built to diverge will diverge, so the question is comparative: how much liking does acting on wanting lose in a cue-heavy, variable-reward environment against a plain one, and how much do a closure ritual and a stopping rule give back? And does the lifetime optimizer of the horizon study avoid the trap?

Interpretation

Acting on wanting is the greedy move, and in the cue-heavy, variable-reward world it costs about half of lifetime liking (48.8% [46.3, 51.4]). About two thirds of that is plain greed: an agent that is greedy on liking already loses 33%. About a third (0.30 of the loss, 15.8 points) is the wanting itself.

In the plain world the wanting part is near zero (0.2 points). The gap a world allows depends on its cues: trigger cues, autoplay at the stop and variable reward together.

The closure ritual (no cue at the natural stop) gives back the whole wanting cost and costs nothing to anyone else. The stopping rule (two units a day) helps the wanting agent less and takes 7% from the lifetime optimizer, who needed no protection.

The lifetime optimizer avoids the trap: it takes 46 units of the cued activity in four weeks against the wanting agent's 105, and keeps a next-day verdict of 7.3 against 2.8. A discounting planner (gamma 0.9) still loses only 6%.

For the evaluator: liking per wanting-driven unit and the next-day verdict separate the cells well. Closure rate separates environments but does not track good outcomes on its own (the optimizer closes only 41% of sessions). The flag also fires in the plain world for 39% of personas, so it is a coarse alarm.

Everything here is a property of assumed numbers in a one-person model. It says nothing yet about households or people.
